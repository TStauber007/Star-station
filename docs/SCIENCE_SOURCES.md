# Star Station — Science Sources

This file lists every science fact used in the Star Station stories. For each one it gives the adult wording, the exact line or lines the children hear (copied word for word from [docs/SCRIPT.md](SCRIPT.md), with the mission page in brackets), and where the fact comes from. Facts are **simplified for preschoolers but never misleading**: a kid line can leave details out, but it must never say something false.

- **Links checked:** 2026-10-17.
- **How they were checked:** each link below came up in a web search on 2026-10-17, with the page's title, address and supporting text, and that text backs up the claim. The build sandbox's network rules blocked opening the pages directly, so a grown-up should click each link once before release to make sure it still opens.
- Most sources are NASA and ESA pages. A few backups come from NOAA, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), Encyclopaedia Britannica, Oregon State University Extension, NASA technical reports (ntrs.nasa.gov), a NASA-funded research paper (PubMed Central) and UC Davis.
- **Star Station is made up.** It is a small, friendly station modelled on the real International Space Station (ISS). The science is about the real ISS; story details such as Button, the Sun Catcher, the party lights and the telescope are invented (see Notes).

## Mission 1 — Welcome Aboard

**F1.1 — Astronauts float inside the space station because the station and everything inside are falling around Earth together (orbit / free fall), not because there's no gravity.**
Kid line (p2):

> Inside the station, everything floats.

> Here's the secret. Our whole station is always falling around the Earth. Swooping around and around, and never landing.

> And everything inside swoops right along with it. So everything floats!

Sources:
- [What Is Microgravity? (Grades K-4) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-k-4/): astronauts float because they are in free fall; the spacecraft and everything in it fall around Earth together without ever hitting the ground. Gravity about 250 miles up is still about 89% as strong as at the surface.
- [What is Microgravity? — NASA Glenn Research Center](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/glenn/what-is-microgravity/): the station is so close to Earth that gravity there is only about 10% weaker; the station, astronaut and objects are "all falling together."
- [What Is Microgravity? (Grades 5-8) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-microgravity-grades-5-8/): calling it "zero gravity" is misleading.

**F1.2 — From space, Earth looks mostly blue because most of Earth's surface (about 71%) is covered by ocean.**
Kid line (p8):

> The Earth was so big, and so blue.

> Most of the Earth is covered with water. That's why it looks so blue!

Sources:
- [How many oceans are there? — NOAA Ocean Service](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/howmanyoceans.html): the ocean "covers 71 percent of the Earth."
- [Weighing Earth's Water from Space — NASA Science (Earth Observatory)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/weighing-earths-water-from-space/): the large amount of water on Earth's surface is what makes Earth look blue; water covers about 70 percent of the surface.
- [Could Earth's light blue color be a signature of life? — NASA Astrobiology](https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/could-earths-light-blue-color-be-a-signature-of-life/): adult detail: the air also scatters blue light (Rayleigh scattering), so the oceans are the main reason, not the only one. The kid line is still fair.

**F1.3 — From the station you can see white clouds over Earth.**
Kid line (p8):

> And those white swirls? Those are clouds.

Sources:
- [Cupola Over the Caribbean — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/147296/cupola-over-the-caribbean): an astronaut's photo from inside the Cupola showing "clouds tracing out the islands of the Caribbean Sea."
- [Cloudscape at Dawn, Northwest Atlantic — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/149490/cloudscape-at-dawn-northwest-atlantic): towering cumulus clouds photographed from the space station.

**F1.4 — Astronauts hold handrails and use foot loops to stay in place and move around; Velcro and straps hold things so they don't float away.**
Kid line (p2; also M5 p6):

> That's why there are handrails to hold on to, and Velcro to keep things from floating away.

> "Somebody forgot to Velcro the spoon!" said Button.

Sources:
- [Crew Restraint Design for the International Space Station — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20060009022): "many of the tasks on ISS are performed with the crew restrained merely by hooking their arms or toes around handrails."
- [Life on board the Space Station — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Celsius_Mission_-_English_version/Life_on_board_the_Space_Station): the station has "numerous restraining loops which astronauts use to anchor their feet."
- [Learning to live with the laws of motion — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Astronauts/Learning_to_live_with_the_laws_of_motion): "Nowadays astronauts keep equipment in place with Velcro or bungee straps."

**F1.5 — The ISS Cupola is an Earth-facing dome with seven windows (one big round window in the middle and six around it). Astronauts look out at Earth through it, and at night they can see and photograph stars.**
Kid line (p8; also M4 p9):

> And there it was. The Cupola! A round room made of windows.

> Seven windows, looking out at the Earth, and the stars.

> Then Button had an idea. "Wait! The Cupola has seven windows. There's room for everyone to see the full Moon, all at once!"

Sources:
- [The Cupola opens its seven eyelids — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Node-3_Cupola/The_Cupola_opens_its_seven_eyelids): the Cupola's seven windows (six side windows around one round top window), each with its own shutter.
- [Cupola — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/cupola/): seven windows, used for watching Earth, spacewalks, and spacecraft arriving and leaving.
- [APOD 2015 January 23: Interior View — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2015-january-23-interior-view/): the Cupola sits on the Earth-facing port of the Tranquility module and gives wide views of Earth.
- [Space Station Star Trail — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/space-station-star-trail/) and [A Trail of Night Lights from the International Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/trail-of-night-lights-from-international-space-station/): star-trail photos made from the station at night (Don Pettit, 2012; Christina Koch, 2019).

**F1.6 — The space station travels very fast, about 17,500 miles per hour (about 5 miles / 8 km every second).**
Kid line (p8):

> Our station zooms around the Earth super, super fast. There's always something new to see.

Sources:
- [Spot the Station Frequently Asked Questions — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/spot-the-station-frequently-asked-questions/): the station travels about 17,500 mph (28,000 km/h) and goes around Earth about once every 90 minutes.
- [ISS 20th anniversary infographic (PDF) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/iss20_infographic_david_20201001.pdf): the crew lives and works "while traveling at a speed of five miles per second."

**F1.7 — Astronauts usually eat tortillas instead of bread in space, because bread makes crumbs that float around and can get into equipment or eyes.**
Kid line (p11):

> In space, we usually eat tortillas instead of bread. Bread makes crumbs, and crumbs float everywhere!

Sources:
- [Food on the International Space Station — NASA History](https://www.nasa.gov/history/space-station-20th-food-on-iss/): tortillas, "unlike regular bread, didn't create crumbs"; bread crumbs could drift through the cabin and harm equipment.
- [NASA Spaceflight Q&A — NASA](https://robotics.nasa.gov/spaceflight-qa/): "crumbs float around in microgravity and can get in crewmembers' eyes or foul air filters and electronic equipment."
- [Space Food — Houston We Have a Podcast, NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/space-food/): a NASA food scientist says the station does get one kind of long-lasting bread, but tortillas are the usual choice. That's why the kid line says "usually."

**F1.8 — Uncrewed cargo spacecraft (such as Russia's Progress and SpaceX's cargo Dragon) dock with the ISS and deliver food, water and other supplies.**
Kid line (p3):

> Bing, bong! A little supply ship had docked with the station.

> It brought food, and water, and... something else.

Sources:
- [International Space Station Visiting Vehicles — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-visiting-vehicles/): lists the spacecraft that visit the station, including the uncrewed Progress resupply ships and SpaceX Dragon cargo craft.
- [Progress 95 Cargo Craft Docks to Station with Food, Fuel, and Supplies — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/04/27/progress-95-cargo-craft-docks-to-station-with-food-fuel-and-supplies/): the uncrewed Progress 95 docked carrying about three tons of food, fuel and supplies.
- [SpaceX Dragon Docks with Science, Supplies, and Roll-Out Solar Arrays — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/10/15/spacex-dragon-docks-with-science-supplies-and-roll-out-solar-arrays/): a cargo Dragon docked on Oct. 15, 2026 with more than 6,300 pounds of food, supplies and equipment.
- [Station Quartet Preps for Departure Amidst Air and Water Quality Checks — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/general-blog/2026/09/29/station-quartet-preps-for-departure-amidst-air-and-water-quality-checks/): a cosmonaut "transferred water from tanks inside the docked Progress 96 cargo spacecraft" into the station.

**F1.9 — Earth spins once a day (about every 24 hours).**
Kid line (end):

> Below them, the Earth turned slowly. Blue, and white, and beautiful.

Sources:
- [What Is Earth? (Grades K-4) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-earth-grades-k-4/): Earth makes one full spin every 24 hours, "We call this a day"; the side facing the Sun has daytime.
- [Day, night, and the seasons — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Lessons/Day_night_and_the_seasons): ESA's kids' lesson on how Earth's spin causes day and night.

## Mission 2 — The Space Garden

**F2.1 — Plants need light and water to grow.**
Kid line (p1):

> "Plants need light to grow," said Button. "Our garden has special red lights and blue lights. Together, they make our garden glow pink and purple!"

> "And plants need water, too. Light and water help plants grow."

Sources:
- [What does life need for survival? — NASA Science (Astrobiology learning resources)](https://science.nasa.gov/astrobiology/learning-resources/alp/what-does-life-need-for-survival/): NASA's early-grades teaching text says "Plants need water and light to live and grow."
- [Growing Plants in Space — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/exploration-research-and-technology/growing-plants-in-space/): in the Veggie garden, a bank of LEDs above the plants gives them the light they need to grow.

**F2.2 — The ISS Veggie garden grows plants under LED lights; red and blue (plus some green) LEDs make the garden glow pinkish-purple.**
Kid line (p1; also end):

> It glows pink and purple, like a sunset in a box.

> "Plants need light to grow," said Button. "Our garden has special red lights and blue lights. Together, they make our garden glow pink and purple!"

> And in its cozy corner, the little garden glowed pink and purple, growing a tiny bit more every day.

Sources:
- [Veggie Plant Growth System Activated on International Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/veggie-plant-growth-system-activated-on-international-space-station/): Veggie has red, blue and green LEDs. Plants reflect a lot of green light and use more red and blue, so the Veggie chamber "typically glows magenta pink."
- [Veggie fact sheet (PDF) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/veggie_fact_sheet_508.pdf): each Veggie chamber has a light array of red, blue and green LEDs.

**F2.3 — Astronauts on the ISS grew red romaine lettuce and ate some (August 2015).**
Kid line (p1):

> "Real astronauts have grown lettuce in space," said Button. "And they ate it, too!"

Sources:
- [Veggie Success Benefits Journey to Mars — NASA Kennedy blog](https://blogs.nasa.gov/kennedy/2015/08/11/veggie-success-benefits-journey-to-mars-and-eaters-on-earth/): on Aug. 10, 2015, Scott Kelly, Kjell Lindgren and Kimiya Yui harvested and tasted "Outredgeous" red romaine lettuce grown in Veggie.
- [Veggie: Space Vegetables for the International Space Station and Beyond — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160004060): the VEG-01B crop was harvested and eaten by the crew on Aug. 10, 2015.

**F2.4 — In Veggie, seeds grow in small "plant pillows" filled with a soil-like clay and fertilizer; astronauts add water carefully, because in microgravity loose water floats around in blobs/balls.**
Kid line (p2, p3):

> In space, seeds grow in little plant pillows. Soft and puffy, and filled with special soil.

> Button gave the water pouch a gentle squeeze. Out came a wobbly, wiggly ball of water!

> "In space, water floats in wobbly little balls," said Button. "So we water our plants very, very carefully."

Sources:
- [Veggie Plant Growth System Activated on International Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/veggie-plant-growth-system-activated-on-international-space-station/): the pillows hold calcined clay (the kind used on baseball fields) and controlled-release fertilizer. "Special soil" is a fair preschool simplification.
- [The Shape of Watering Plants in Space — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/the-shape-of-watering-plants-in-space/): astronauts water the plant pillows by hand with a syringe; getting water to behave in microgravity is the hard part.
- [Flight Engineer Jessica Meir observes a floating sphere of water — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/flight-engineer-jessica-meir-observes-floating-sphere-of-water/): a photo of a floating ball of water inside the station.

**F2.5 — In space, plant leaves and stems grow toward the light (light helps plants know which way to grow when there's no "down").**
Kid line (p8):

> And look! Little green plants, reaching up toward the lights.

> "See how the leaves grow toward the light?" said Button. "In space, light helps plants know which way to grow."

Sources:
- [Growing Plants in Space — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/exploration-research-and-technology/growing-plants-in-space/): "in the absence of gravity, plants use other environmental factors, such as light, to orient and guide growth."

**F2.6 — Seeds sprout within days, but lettuce takes weeks (about a month) to grow big enough to eat.**
Kid line (p4, p7, end):

> "Seeds take time," said Button. "Lots and lots of time."

> A few sleeps went by.

> Every day, the crew gave the plants a little drink. And for weeks and weeks, the plants grew a tiny bit more every day.

> At last, the lettuce was ready. Abigail and Tommy took a big bite. Crunch!

Sources:
- [Veggie: Space Vegetables for the International Space Station and Beyond — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160004060): in the first Veggie test, red romaine lettuce grew on the station for 33 days before harvest.
- [Soil temperature conditions for vegetable seed germination — Oregon State University Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/gardening/soil-compost/soil-temperature-conditions-vegetable-seed-germination): lettuce seedlings come up in about 3 days at around 68°F (about 7 days in cold soil).
- Resolved: the first sprout now comes after "a few sleeps", and the "weeks and weeks" come between sprouting and harvest.

**F2.7 — Astronauts have grown zinnia flowers on the ISS (they bloomed in the Veggie garden in January 2016).**
Kid line (p1 card, p2):

> Or bright orange flowers, called zinnias?

> Zinnias it is! Real astronauts have grown zinnia flowers in space, too.

Sources:
- [First Flower Grown in Space Station's Veggie Facility — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/first-flower-grown-space-stations-veggie-facility-2/): on Jan. 16, 2016, Commander Scott Kelly shared photos of a blooming zinnia in the Veggie garden.
- [Zinnia Flowers from International Space Station Yield Seeds for Future Experiments — NASA Kennedy blog](https://blogs.nasa.gov/kennedy/2016/07/22/zinnia-flowers-from-international-space-station-yield-seeds-for-future-experiments/): the experiment began Nov. 16, 2015; the flowers were harvested Feb. 14, 2016 and sent back to Earth.
- [ZINNIAS FROM SPACE! — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/zinnias-from-space-nasa-studies-the-multiple-benefits-of-gardening/): Scott Kelly cleaned mold off the overwatered zinnias and nursed the survivors back until they flowered.
- No source checked the colour of the space zinnias. "Bright orange" is a story detail (zinnias come in many colours, orange among them).

**F2.8 — When a seed starts to grow, a tiny root usually comes out first. In space there is no "down" for roots to follow; light, water and food in the growing material guide them instead.**
Kid line (p5-peek, p5-rest):

> And there was the seed! It had a teeny, tiny root, as thin as a hair. It was just starting to grow.

> Tiny roots were starting to grow, deeper and deeper into the pillow.

Sources:
- [Germination — Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/germination): growth "usually" begins when the primary root (radicle) pushes out of the seed.
- [Root waving and skewing — unexpectedly in micro-g (Paul et al., 2012) — PubMed Central](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3533921/): on the ISS, roots with no gravity to follow grew away from the overhead LED light.
- [Growing Plants in Space — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/exploration-research-and-technology/growing-plants-in-space/): without gravity, plants rely on other cues, such as light, to orient their growth.
- Resolved: the line no longer says "down".

## Mission 3 — The Wobbly Panel

**F3.1 — Solar panels turn sunlight into electricity.**
Kid line (p1):

> Solar panels turn sunshine into power.

Sources:
- [Solar — Energy Kids, U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/kids/energy-sources/solar/): solar (photovoltaic) cells turn sunlight directly into electricity.
- [What Powers a Spacecraft? — NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-powers-a-spacecraft/en/): spacecraft near Earth carry solar panels that turn sunlight into electricity.

**F3.2 — Big solar arrays ("wings") make the electricity the station uses (lights, computers, etc.).**
Kid line (p1):

> Star Station has two big, shiny wings. They're made of solar panels.

> Power for the lights. Power for the computers. And power for Button!

Sources:
- [New Solar Arrays to Power NASA's International Space Station Research — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/new-solar-arrays-to-power-nasas-international-space-station-research/): the eight original arrays make up to 160 kilowatts in daylight, about half of which goes to the batteries. (The real ISS has eight big array wings; made-up Star Station has two.)
- [Overview of International Space Station Electrical Power System (PDF) — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160014034/downloads/20160014034.pdf): the arrays feed eight power channels, "one per solar array wing," so "wings" is NASA's own word.
- [Station Solar Arrays — Houston We Have a Podcast, NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/station-solar-arrays/): array power runs the station's systems, including core systems, pumps and fans.

**F3.3 — Solar panels make the most power when they face the Sun, so the station's solar arrays turn to follow the Sun.**
Kid line (p7):

> "Solar panels work best when they face the Sun," said Button. "Out there, the station's big wings turn to face the Sun."

Sources:
- [Station Solar Arrays — Houston We Have a Podcast, NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/station-solar-arrays/): beta gimbals point each array toward the Sun, and the big solar alpha rotary joint "turns all of the arrays a full 360 degrees around every orbit."
- [The International Space Station Solar Alpha Rotary Joint (PDF) — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100021920/downloads/20100021920.pdf): the joint turns the arrays to follow the Sun, one full turn per orbit (about every 90 minutes).
- [Solar photovoltaic output depends on orientation, tilt, and tracking — U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=18871): panels "capture more solar energy when they are pointed directly at the sun."

**F3.4 — When the station is in Earth's shadow, it runs on batteries that the solar arrays charged in sunlight.**
Kid line (p10, end):

> "Nope!" said Button. "In the dark, the station uses batteries. Batteries that our solar wings filled up in the sunshine."

> The station lights glowed warm and cozy, powered by sunshine saved up for later.

Sources:
- [Solar Arrays on the International Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/solar-arrays-international-space-station-2/): about 60 percent of the arrays' power recharges the batteries in sunlight; the batteries power the station during the dark part of each orbit.
- [New Solar Arrays to Power NASA's International Space Station Research — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/new-solar-arrays-to-power-nasas-international-space-station-research/): about half the daytime power is stored in batteries for when the station is not in sunlight.
- [What Powers a Spacecraft? — NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-powers-a-spacecraft/en/): solar power charges onboard batteries that keep a spacecraft running when it's in shadow.

**F3.5 — From the station, sunset is the Sun dropping behind the edge (horizon) of the Earth as the station flies into Earth's shadow. It happens about every 90 minutes, with bands of colour along Earth's edge.**
Kid line (p10; also M5 p1):

> Just then, the station sailed into Earth's shadow. Outside, it got dark.

> Through the window, the Sun slipped behind the edge of the Earth.

> Tommy, can you help the sunset? Tap, tap, tap, and watch the colors change!

Sources:
- [APOD 2010 June 23: Sunset from the International Space Station — NASA](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap100623.html): the Expedition 23 crew photographed a sunset over Earth's edge, with the lowest layer of air glowing deep orange and yellow and a light blue band above it.
- [The colours of orbit — ESA](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2015/03/The_colours_of_orbit): from orbit the Sun disappears fast (two photos taken two minutes apart show it vanishing); low sunlight passing through more air looks red and orange.
- [APOD 2008 February 28: ISS: Sunlight to Shadow — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2008-february-28-iss-sunlight-to-shadow/): the station slides into Earth's shadow once every orbit (about every 90 minutes).

## Mission 4 — Turns at the Telescope

*The telescope in this mission is a story invention (see Notes). The Moon facts below are real.*

**F4.1 — The Moon doesn't make its own light; it reflects sunlight.**
Kid line (p5):

> "The Moon doesn't make its own light," said Button. "It shines with light from the Sun."

Sources:
- [Moon Phases — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/): the Moon makes no light of its own; "moonlight" is reflected sunlight.
- [What Are the Moon's Phases? — NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/moon-phases/en/): the Sun lights the Moon; without it the Moon would be completely dark.

**F4.2 — Moon phases happen because, as the Moon travels around Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half.**
Kid line (p5, p6-chart):

> "And as the Moon goes around the Earth, we see more of its sunny side, or less. So it looks a little different, night after night."

> "The Moon isn't really growing," said Button. "We just see more of its sunny side."

Sources:
- [What Are the Moon's Phases? — NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/moon-phases/en/): the Sun always lights half of the Moon, and how much of that half we see changes as the Moon goes around Earth.
- [Moon Phases — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/): phases come from our changing view of the half-lit Moon, not from Earth's shadow.

**F4.3 — A full cycle of phases takes about a month (about 29.5 days).**
Kid line (p6):

> It takes the Moon about a month to go through all its shapes, from dark, to full, and back again.

Sources:
- [Moon Phases — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/): the cycle repeats about every 29.5 days.
- [Moon Facts — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/): the Moon takes about 27.3 days to orbit Earth but 29.5 days to go from new moon to new moon.

**F4.4 — Phase names: new, crescent, quarter ("half moon"), gibbous, full.**
Kid line (p5, p6, p7):

> The Moon was a thin, curvy sliver. Like a smile. Like a banana!

> "That's called a crescent moon," said Button.

> A thin crescent. Then a half moon. Then a big, bulgy, almost-full moon.

> And then, one night, the Moon was round, and full, and bright as a lamp.

Sources:
- [Moon Phases — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/): the eight phases, in order, are new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, third quarter and waning crescent. At first quarter we see half of the sunlit side, which people often call a "half moon." "Gibbous" means more than half lit but less than full, which the script calls "big, bulgy, almost-full."

**F4.5 — The same side of the Moon, with its dark patches (the maria, old lava plains), always faces Earth.**
Kid line (p9):

> "See the dark patches?" said Button. "The Moon always shows us that same face."

Sources:
- [Moon Facts — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/): the Moon is tidally locked, spinning exactly once for each trip around Earth, so people on Earth only ever see one side.
- [The Near Side of the Moon — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/the-near-side-of-the-moon/): the dark patches on the side facing Earth are ancient lava flows.

**F4.6 — There is one Moon. People all over Earth see the same Moon, with the same phase on a given night.**
Kid line (p9-share):

> A message crackled back from Mission Control. "What a beautiful Moon! We can see it from down here, too."

> People all over the world can look up, and see the very same Moon.

> One Moon, for everybody to share.

Sources:
- [Top Moon Questions — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/top-moon-questions/): "Are Moon phases the same everywhere on Earth?" Yes, everyone sees the same phase; from the other hemisphere it just looks upside down.
- [Moon Facts — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/): Earth has one Moon, which orbits the whole planet.
- Adult nuance: the kid line says people *can* see it. At any one moment only the side of Earth facing the Moon can see it, and Earth's turning gives everyone a turn.

**F4.7 — From the station, the Moon can be seen rising over the edge (horizon) of the Earth.**
Kid line (p1):

> Tonight, something special was going to rise over the edge of the Earth.

> And there, peeking over the edge of the Earth, was the Moon.

Sources:
- [Moon Rise From the Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/moon-rise-from-space-station/): NASA astronaut Randy Bresnik photographed the rising Moon over Earth's horizon from the station (August 2017).
- [Moonrise from space — ESA](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2011/01/Moonrise_from_space): ESA astronaut Paolo Nespoli's photos of moonrise over the South Pacific, taken from the station.
- [Five Minutes in Orbit — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/154728/five-minutes-in-orbit): a time-lapse from the station in which a crescent Moon rises in the middle of the scene.

## Mission 5 — Night-Side Noises

**F5.1 — The ISS goes around Earth about every 90 minutes.**
Kid line (p1):

> Star Station zooms around the Earth so fast, it goes all the way around in about an hour and a half.

Sources:
- [Spot the Station Frequently Asked Questions — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/spot-the-station-frequently-asked-questions/): the station goes around Earth about once every 90 minutes.
- [Space Station Orbit Tutorial — NASA Johnson Space Center](https://esrs.jsc.nasa.gov/Tools/orbitTutorial.htm): each orbit takes 90–93 minutes, depending on the station's height.

**F5.2 — So the crew sees about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day.**
Kid line (p1):

> That means a sunrise, and a sunset, again and again. Astronauts see about sixteen sunrises every single day!

Sources:
- [International Space Station Facts and Figures — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/): the station makes 16 orbits in 24 hours, passing through 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets.
- [Sleeping in space — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Life_in_Space/Living_in_space/Sleeping_in_space): "There are 16 sunsets and sunrises every 24 hours on the ISS."

**F5.3 — On the night side, the station is in Earth's shadow; astronauts can see city lights on Earth below.**
Kid line (p1, p9):

> The station sailed into Earth's shadow, on the night side of the world.

> Back at the big window, the Earth was dark. But not all dark.

> Down below, tiny lights glowed, gold and white. "Cities," whispered Button. "Where people are sleeping."

Sources:
- [APOD 2008 February 28: ISS: Sunlight to Shadow — NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2008-february-28-iss-sunlight-to-shadow/): the station slides into Earth's shadow once every orbit.
- [Cities at Night: The View from Space — NASA Science (Earth Observatory)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/cities-at-night-the-view-from-space/): astronauts photograph city lights from the space station at night.
- [Windy City of Lights — NASA Science (Earth Observatory)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/windy-city-of-lights-153622/): "Orange, yellow, and white lights span Chicago at night" (supports "gold and white").

**F5.4 — The ISS is noisy inside: fans and pumps run all the time. Fans are needed because in microgravity warm air doesn't rise, so air doesn't mix on its own; fans keep fresh air moving (for example, so breathed-out carbon dioxide doesn't collect around a sleeping astronaut's head).**
Kid line (p3, end):

> "Oh! That's a fan," said Button. "In space, air doesn't swirl around by itself, like it does on Earth. So fans keep the fresh air blowing, all day and all night."

> Hmmmmmm, hummed the fans. Gurgle, said the water machine.

Sources:
- [Acoustics and Noise Control — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/acoustics-and-noise-control/): the ISS has many systems "with their associated fans, pumps, valves, and other mechanical noises."
- [Daily life — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Astronauts/Daily_life): "In weightlessness the warm air exhaled can not go 'up' so it settles in a bubble around the astronaut"; without fans this would lead to carbon dioxide build-up. The fans are noisy.
- [Sleeping in space — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Life_in_Space/Living_in_space/Sleeping_in_space): without moving air, a bubble of an astronaut's own breathed-out carbon dioxide can form around their head.

**F5.5 — Orbital night (time in Earth's shadow) lasts at most about 35–36 minutes, then the Sun rises again. On a few days a year the station gets no night at all.**
Kid line (p9; also M3 end):

> Night on the station doesn't last long. Soon, in less than an hour, the Sun comes back.

> It wouldn't have to wait long.

Sources:
- [The Electric Power System of the International Space Station, NASA/TM-2000-210209 (PDF) — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000063506/downloads/20000063506.pdf): depending on the Sun angle, the station gets anywhere from 35 minutes of eclipse per orbit to almost the whole 90 minutes in sunlight.
- [Overview of International Space Station Electrical Power System (PDF) — NASA Technical Reports Server](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160014034/downloads/20160014034.pdf): "90 minute orbit, 30 minute eclipse."
- [From Day Into Night on the International Space Station — NASA](https://nasa.gov/image-feature/from-day-into-night-on-the-international-space-station): a couple of times a year the orbit lines up with Earth's day/night line and the station stays in sunlight the whole time.

**F5.6 — The ISS has a water recovery machine that cleans and recycles water (from humidity, sweat and urine) so the crew can use and drink it again.**
Kid line (p8):

> "It's the water machine!" said Button. "It cleans our water, so we can use it again and again."

Sources:
- [NASA Achieves Water Recovery Milestone on International Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/nasa-achieves-water-recovery-milestone-on-international-space-station/): the system now recovers about 98% of the crew's water. It collects moisture from breath and sweat out of the cabin air, distills urine, and purifies the water into drinking water.
- [Water Recovery System Racks — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Node-3_Cupola/Water_Recovery_System_Racks): the Water Recovery System racks on the station's Tranquility module recycle the crew's water. This older page gives a lower recovery figure than today's 98%.

**F5.7 — Orbital sunrise: a thin band of orange-red light appears along Earth's edge, with blue layers of air above it, before the Sun itself comes up.**
Kid line (p9):

> A thin line of orange light glowed along the edge of the Earth. Then gold. Then bright, bright blue.

> Sunrise!

Sources:
- [Sunrise from the Station — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/152259/sunrise-from-the-station): an astronaut's photo of sunrise over Earth's edge; the lowest layer of air (the troposphere) glows orange-red, with the blue stratosphere above it.
- [APOD 2010 June 23: Sunset from the International Space Station — NASA](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap100623.html): the same layers of colour (deep orange and yellow low down, light blue above) at the other end of the night.

## Mission 6 — Goodnight, Earth

**F6.1 — Astronauts sleep in sleeping bags attached/strapped to the wall of small sleeping cabins so they don't float around.**
Kid line (p3):

> In space, we sleep in sleeping bags tied to the wall, so we don't float away while we dream.

Sources:
- [Sleeping in space — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Life_in_Space/Living_in_space/Sleeping_in_space): "Astronauts can attach their sleeping bags to a wall or a ceiling."
- [Astronaut selection 2021-22 FAQs — ESA](https://www.esa.int/About_Us/Careers_at_ESA/ESA_Astronaut_Selection/Astronaut_selection_2021-22_FAQs): each astronaut has their own crew cabin with a sleeping bag strapped to the wall so they don't float around.

**F6.2 — In microgravity there's no up or down, so astronauts can sleep in any direction.**
Kid line (p3):

> "In space, there's no up or down," said Button. "You can sleep any way you like."

Sources:
- [Sleeping in space — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Life_in_Space/Living_in_space/Sleeping_in_space): "There is no up or down, and everything is weightless."
- [Space Sleep — NASA Human Space Flight](https://spaceflight.nasa.gov/living/spacesleep/index.html): astronauts are weightless and "can sleep in any orientation," but they must attach themselves so they don't drift into things.

**F6.3 — Astronauts sometimes use eye masks and earplugs to sleep (frequent sunrises; noisy fans).**
Kid line (p4):

> Button put on a little sleep mask. "Some astronauts wear eye masks," said Button, "to keep the sunrises out."

Sources:
- [Living and Working in Space fact sheet (PDF) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/167746main_fs_livingandworkinginspace508c.pdf): each astronaut gets "a sleep mask and ear plugs to block out noise and light."
- [A good night's sleep in orbit — ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/A_good_night_s_sleep_in_orbit): the crew may wear eyeshades or pull shutters over the windows "to keep out the sunlight while they are sleeping"; some sleep with earplugs.

**F6.4 — From the station astronauts can see auroras: glowing ribbons of (often green) light near Earth's poles. The green glow is about 100–250 km up, below the station's roughly 400 km orbit.**
Kid line (p7):

> Out the little window, far below, ribbons of green light began to dance, over the top of the world.

> "An aurora," whispered Button. "Astronauts can see them from up here."

Sources:
- [What Is an Aurora? — NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/aurora/en/): particles from the Sun are guided toward Earth's polar regions, where they make the air glow; oxygen gives green and red light.
- [Green Aurora Seen from the Space Station — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/1920/green-aurora-seen-from-the-space-station): green aurora photographed from the ISS; the green comes from oxygen about 100–250 km up.
- [Observing our Planet from Low Earth Orbit — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/iss-research/observing-our-planet-from-low-earth-orbit/): the station orbits about 400 km up, so green aurora really is "far below."
- [Morning Aurora From the Space Station — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-feature/morning-aurora-from-the-space-station): Scott Kelly photographed "the green lights of the aurora" from the station on Oct. 7, 2015.

**F6.5 — At night, city lights on Earth glow gold (sodium lamps) and white (LEDs) when seen from the station.**
Kid line (p7; also end):

> And all across the dark Earth, the cities glowed like tiny sparkles of gold and white.

> Goodnight, little city lights. Goodnight, stars.

Sources:
- [Windy City of Lights — NASA Science (Earth Observatory)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/windy-city-of-lights-153622/): "Orange, yellow, and white lights span Chicago at night." Older sodium lamps glow warm orange; newer LEDs look whiter or bluer.
- [Tokyo at Night — NASA Earth Observatory](https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/images/8683/tokyo-at-night): Tokyo looks blue-green from orbit because it uses more mercury-vapor lamps, while sodium lamps glow orange-yellow.
- [Cities at Night: The View from Space — NASA Science (Earth Observatory)](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/cities-at-night-the-view-from-space/): astronaut photos of city lights taken from the station.

**F6.6 — In microgravity, being "upside down" doesn't matter; there's no up or down.**
Kid line (p4):

> "In space, upside down is just another way up," said Button.

Sources:
- [Upside down or right way up? — ESA](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2015/10/Upside_down_or_right_way_up): an astronaut's brain can feel "upside down," "but in space there is no up or down."
- [Sleeping in space — ESA Space for Kids](https://www.esa.int/kids/en/learn/Life_in_Space/Living_in_space/Sleeping_in_space): "There is no up or down, and everything is weightless."

**F6.7 — On the ISS, drinks come in sealed pouches. Water is added through a valve, and astronauts sip through a straw that can be clamped shut, so the liquid doesn't escape as floating blobs.**
Kid line (p2-drink):

> In space, drinks come in pouches, with a straw. That way, the water doesn't float away in wobbly balls.

Sources:
- [Space Food — Houston We Have a Podcast, NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/space-food/): drinks are sipped through a straw, and a clamp on the straw stops a bubble of liquid from coming out and floating around between sips.
- [Space Food fact sheet (PDF) — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/167750main_fs_spacefood508c.pdf): drinks come as dry mixes in pouches; water is added through a valve and a straw goes in for drinking.
- [Sixth SpaceX Delivery of Station Research With a Side of Caffeine — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/sixth-spacex-delivery-of-station-research-with-side-of-caffeine/): in microgravity liquids cling to the walls of cups, "making these plastic pouches and straws necessary."

**F6.8 — Mission Control on Earth talks to the crew by radio. The station's antennas send radio signals through relay satellites (NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellites) to the ground and on to Houston, with no noticeable delay.**
Kid line (p2-drink; also M2 end, M4 p9-share):

> Then a soft crackle came from the radio. It was Mission Control, calling from the Earth.

> A message crackled in from Mission Control, far below on the Earth. "Beautiful garden, Star Station!"

Sources:
- [The Space Network: Cell Towers for Astronauts — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/the-space-network-cell-towers-for-astronauts/): an antenna on the station sends a radio signal to a relay satellite, then to White Sands, New Mexico, and on to Houston, "in milliseconds so that there is no noticeable delay in communication."
- [The International Space Station: 20 Years of Communications Excellence — NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/the-international-space-station-20-years-of-communications-excellence/): the station keeps in near-constant contact with Mission Control through relay satellites, with a backup VHF radio for emergencies.

## Notes and corrections

No fact was false. These are the facts whose kid line or adult wording changed, or that need a note:

1. **Star Station is made up.** It is a small, friendly station modelled on the real ISS, not the ISS itself. Story details are invented: two solar wings (the real ISS has eight big array wings), the Sun Catcher panel, the colour-changing party lights, the glow-in-the-dark stars and Button the robot. The science facts in this file describe the real ISS.
2. **The telescope in Mission 4 is a story invention.** The real ISS has no telescope for the crew to look at the Moon. Astronauts look out of the Cupola windows and photograph Earth, the Moon and stars with cameras and long zoom lenses. For example, [Cities at Night: The View from Space](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/cities-at-night-the-view-from-space/) describes night photos taken with a 400 mm lens. Saying a telescope makes the Moon "look big, and close" is true of telescopes in general.
3. **F1.5 (Cupola):** "all looking down at Earth" became "looking out at the Earth, and the stars." The Cupola faces Earth, but its side windows are angled and also show the horizon and space. Stars are visible from the station at night.
4. **F1.7 (tortillas):** the line says "usually." Tortillas are the normal choice, but the station does get one kind of long-lasting bread.
5. **F2.2 (garden lights):** the line says the *garden* glows pink and purple, not "everything." The real Veggie chamber also has some green LEDs; leaving those out is a fair simplification.
6. **F2.4 (plant pillows):** "filled with special soil" is fine for preschoolers. The real material is a baked clay that works like soil.
7. **F2.7 (zinnias):** NASA calls it the first flower grown in the *Veggie* garden, not the first flower ever grown in space. Don't say "first flower in space." The orange colour is a story choice that wasn't checked against a source.
8. **F3.3 (panels turn to the Sun):** the flower comparison was dropped. Only *young* sunflowers follow the Sun; grown sunflower heads stop moving and face east ([UC Davis — Sunflowers Move by the Clock](https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/sunflowers-move-clock); [UC Davis — Why Sunflowers Face East](https://www.ucdavis.edu/curiosity/news/why-sunflowers-face-east)). The line now says panels "work best" facing the Sun, because they still make some power at an angle.
9. **F5.4 (fans):** the line says air doesn't "swirl around by itself, like it does on Earth," not "doesn't move." Air in orbit still drifts a little; what's missing is warm air rising, which keeps air mixing on Earth.
10. **F5.5 (orbital night):** the adult wording says night lasts at most about 35–36 minutes, and on a few days a year there's no night at all. Some NASA and ESA pages round this to "about 45 minutes of darkness," which is a simplification. The kid line "Soon, in less than an hour, the Sun comes back" is always true.
11. **F6.5 (city lights):** "gold and white," not just "golden." Older sodium lamps glow gold-orange; newer LED-lit areas look white or bluish.
12. **F1.2 (blue Earth):** NOAA says 71%, NASA usually says "about 70%." The air's blue scattering also helps, but the oceans are the main reason.
13. **F4.6 (same Moon):** only the side of Earth facing the Moon can see it at any one moment; "can look up" covers this.
14. **Flags raised on the script, all fixed in the stories on 2026-10-17:**
    - **M2 p5-rest**, "Tiny roots were starting to grow. Down, down, down." In space there is no "down" for roots (F2.8, F6.2). Now: "Tiny roots were starting to grow, deeper and deeper into the pillow."
    - **M2 p7 → p7-sprout**, "Lots and lots of sleeps went by. Weeks and weeks." comes before the *first* sprout. Lettuce and zinnia seeds sprout within days; the weeks come between sprouting and harvest (F2.6). Now: "A few sleeps went by." there, and "for weeks and weeks" after the sprout.
    - **M1 p2**, "Abigail's hair floats up, up, up." and **M6 p2**, "A pajama leg floated up, like a balloon." In orbit nothing floats "up." Hair spreads out all around the head, and things drift every which way; balloons rise on Earth for a different reason (lighter than air). Now: "Abigail's hair floats out in every direction." and "A pajama leg floated off, all by itself."
    - Optional, **M2 p5-peek**, "the little root began to droop." Drooping hints at gravity; out of its wet pillow, a root would dry out. Now: "the little root began to dry out."
15. **Checking method:** every link here came up in web searches on 2026-10-17, with text that supports the fact. The pages could not be opened directly from the build sandbox. Click through each link once before release.
